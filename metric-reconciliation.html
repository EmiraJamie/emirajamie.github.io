When 130% isn’t an error but is still the wrong answer

A collections report showed a rate of 130%. It looked broken, but the math was right. The real problem was that the metric didn’t measure what people assumed it did.

The problem

A collections rate is a ratio: dollars collected divided by the collectible opportunity. When one came back at 130%, leaders had two bad options. They could write the report off as broken, or they could take the number at face value and coach on it.

What I did

  • Traced it to the source. I traced the figure back to the source data to check both the numerator and the denominator.
  • Found the cause. The arithmetic was correct. In some situations, the payments credited could exceed the opportunity amount the metric used as its denominator, so a result over 100% was mathematically possible.
  • Separated the math from the meaning. I explained to leaders what the metric was measuring, where it stopped matching the business question they were asking, and how to read results like this one.

The result

Leaders didn’t act on a misleading number, and they kept trusting the report instead of discarding it.

What I took from it: validating data means more than checking the formula. A number can be calculated correctly and still answer a different question than the one being asked.
