When 130% isn’t an error but is still the wrong answer

A collections call showed a 130% result when my own math said it should be about 85%. Tracing why exposed a gap between how the metric was documented and how the report actually measured it. That gave leaders a new lever to coach on: collections rose about 9% and credits fell about 20%.

The problem

While trending collections for my team, I spot-checked individual accounts, reading the memos and billing history to see what actually happened, especially on calls with little or no payment. One call showed a collection rate of 130%. The transaction report listed a lower past-due amount than the account showed on the date of the call. By my math, it should have been about 85%: still a great call, but nowhere near 130%.

What I did

  • Got second opinions. A peer leader worked through it with me, and several other leaders I asked agreed that, based on how we understood the metric to work, about 85% should have been correct.
  • Ruled out timing. We waited a couple of days for the data to settle. Nothing changed.
  • Went to the source. I contacted the team that owned the report. Their answer: everything was accurate.
  • Kept watching. Then a similar case turned up on a call with no payment, where the rep’s score took less of a hit than it should have. That’s when it clicked.

What was really happening

What stumped us was a gap between the documentation and the report. Our metric documentation said the rate was based on the customer’s past-due balance when they called, and for suspended accounts, the billing view in our CRM included restore fees in that balance. But restore fees weren’t actually charged until service was restored, and the report measured each call against the balance before those fees.

For example, the CRM might show $160 past due, including three restore fees, while the report measured the call against the $95 owed before the fees. The report was working as built. It just didn’t measure the metric the way the documentation, and every leader we asked, understood it.

What changed

Reps often waived restore fees to get a customer to pay. Now we could see how much that habit was really costing us. Leaders walked their teams through it, and the guidance changed: reps could still negotiate, but they needed to be firmer and waive one or two restore fees at most unless policy said otherwise.

The result

After we took it to our teams, collections rose about 9% and credits fell about 20%.

What I took from it: reporting and documentation don’t always match. When they don’t, people make decisions based on a version of the metric that doesn’t exist, so it’s important that how a metric is calculated is clearly communicated.
